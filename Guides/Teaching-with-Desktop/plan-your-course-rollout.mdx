Start with a topic you already teach. Use the Desktop X-ray Simulator to let students explore how their choices affect an X-ray image, then discuss what they noticed.

For a first introduction, we suggest demonstrating an activity and then letting students try it in pairs. Begin with one class and use the experience to plan the next activity.

## What could this look like in your course?

Imagine you are teaching wrist radiography. Students have learned the anatomy and the coverage required for a PA wrist image. You now want them to explain how collimation affects which anatomy appears.

The simulator gives you a practical comparison to discuss: students save an image, narrow the field until some of the required anatomy is excluded, and save a second image. They identify what is missing and explain the correction they would make.

You could build this into the course as follows:

| When | How you use the simulator |
| --- | --- |
| During your teaching on PA wrist imaging | Show the healthy wrist case. Ask students to predict what will happen as you narrow the field, then discuss the result. |
| At the next practical class | Students work in pairs through the same comparison. Each student takes a turn using the controls and explaining an image. |
| Before moving to the next topic | Review their images and short explanations. Address any confusion about coverage, then use the same approach with another topic you teach. |

[See the wrist collimation activity](/Guides/Teaching-with-Desktop/activities-and-templates#wrist-collimation-what-is-missing) for a suggested class plan, student instructions, and discussion questions.

## Choose a starting point that fits your teaching

**Have computers available in class?** Start with guided practice. Demonstrate the task, then let students work individually or in pairs while you help and discuss their results.

**Have one computer and a projector?** Run the activity as a class demonstration. Ask students to predict each change and explain the resulting image. They can take notes or discuss their answers with a partner.

**Want students to practise between classes?** First introduce the controls in class and check that everyone has access to a suitable computer. Then give them a specific task and tell them what to submit. For example: two images and a short explanation of the difference.

Choose the arrangement your students can access. If some cannot run the simulator on their own computers, arrange time on an institution computer before assigning work to do independently.

## Make a simple plan for the first class

Decide:

- **What students should learn.** Choose one question they should be able to answer, such as "Which anatomy is missing from this image, and what would you change?"
- **Where the activity fits.** Put it after the relevant anatomy and imaging principles have been taught.
- **What students will hand in.** For the wrist example, ask for two images and a few sentences explaining the difference.
- **How you will respond.** You could discuss selected images in the next class or comment on the work through your usual assignment system.

If several instructors teach the course, agree on the activity, the instructions students receive, and who will discuss or review their work. A short note in your existing course plan is enough to keep these decisions together.

## Decide what to do next

After the first class, look at a few image comparisons and ask students what they found difficult.

Use what you learn to choose the next step:

- If students struggled with the controls, give them another guided activity.
- If they could use the simulator but could not explain their images, revisit that concept in class.
- If they could complete and explain the task, try the approach with the next suitable course topic.

Also note whether the activity fit the available time and whether anyone had trouble getting access. Address those issues before introducing it to another class.
